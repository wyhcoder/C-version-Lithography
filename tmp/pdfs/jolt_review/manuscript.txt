               Summary of the Work

               This paper presents a novel and general framework for parametric curvilinear mask
               optimization in optical lithography, with a key focus on deriving the rigorous gradient of the
               objective function with respect to design variables — a significant technical challenge that has
               not been adequately addressed in prior parametric-curve-based mask optimization research.

               Key Contributions

               1. Rigorous Gradient Derivation: We establish three essential links to obtain the rigorous
               gradient: (a) the design velocity linking design variable variation to mask contour variation via
               the analytical parametric curve; (b) the indicator function linking mask contour variation to
               mask transmittance variation; and (c) the contour integration of the product between gradient
               density and design velocity. This rigorous approach fundamentally improves upon existing
               methods that rely on gradient-free optimization or approximate EPE-based gradients.

               2. Efficient Design Variable Strategy: By optimizing only the normal displacements of online
               knots and reconstructing offline knots, we halve the number of design variables while
               maintaining curve smoothness, enabling efficient gradient-based optimization.

               3. General Applicability: The proposed framework is not limited to Bézier curves — it can be
               applied to any parametric curve representation (e.g., B-spline, NURBS) and can even
               accommodate Manhattan-style masks.

               4. Impressive Experimental Results: Using the ICCAD 2013 benchmark masks (scaled 1.25×,
               average CD = 87 nm, area = 2.36 μm²):
               - Each OPC completes in approximately 2 seconds
               - Average maximum edge placement error (EPE) is below 3 nm
               - The method significantly outperforms pixel-based methods in both pattern fidelity (avg. max
               EPE 2.59 nm vs. 9.35 nm) and convergence behavior

               Significance

               As the semiconductor industry moves toward smaller nodes and curvilinear mask geometries
become increasingly important, the ability to perform fast, accurate, and manufacturable mask
optimization is critical. Our framework provides a mathematically rigorous approach that
combines the geometric flexibility of parametric curves with the efficiency of gradient-based
optimization. The results demonstrate strong potential for full-chip applications, with estimated
throughput of 100 mm² wafer area per 24 hours using 1000 CPUs.

We believe that this work represents a meaningful advancement in inverse lithography and
optical proximity correction, and will be of great interest to researchers and engineers in the
field of computational lithography.

             Abstract

             A general framework is proposed for the parametric curvilinear mask optimization with rigorous
             gradient.    As an example of parametric curve, the piecewise cubic Bézier curve is employed
             to represent the curvilinear contour of mask. By optimizing only the normal displacements of
             online knots and reconstructing the offline knots, the number of design variables is reduced while
             maintaining the smoothness of curve. Rigorous gradient of the objective function is derived, in
             which continuum-based formulations of design velocity and indicator function provide important
             links between the variation of design variable and the variation of mask transmittance. Gradient
             based optimization with simple strategy of adaptive step size is implemented. In addition, the
             methods of rasterization and target smoothing are presented. The proposed framework can be
             employed for any parametric curvilinear mask and even for Manhattan-style mask. Experiments
             with masks whose average critical dimension is 87 nm and area is 2.36 µm2 demonstrate that the
             optimization can be completed around 2 seconds and the average of maximum edge placement
             error is below 3 nm.

             1. Introduction

                Lithography is a critical step in integrated circuit manufacturing, aiming to faithfully transfer
             circuit patterns from mask to wafer. However, as critical dimension continues to shrink and device
             density keeps increasing, the optical proximity effect becomes increasingly prominent, leading to
             severe distortion of the transferred patterns and narrow process window. Under such circumstances,
             conventional mask optimization (MO) methods based on Manhattan-style mask exhibit serious

limitations, and those based on curvilinear mask have become an important trend [1–4]. The
curvilinear masks are composed of 2D shapes enclosed by smooth curves, rather than polygons in
the Manhattan-style masks, thus offering higher flexibility in geometric description and a broader
solution space for MO. As a result, it can significantly improve the fidelity of transferred pattern
and extend the process window [1, 2].
   According to the approach employed to represent the curvilinear mask, there are the pixel-
based method, the level-set-based method, and the parametric-curve-based method for curvilinear
MO. The representation approach and the corresponding gradient analysis not only determine
the flexibility in changing the mask layout and the size of solution space, but also influences and
efficiency of optimization.
   In the pixel-based mask optimization [5–10], the mask is represented as a 2D array of pixels,
and the value of pixel represents the transmission in a small region. The pixel-based optimization
of binary mask is essentially a 0-1 integer optimization problem, and a relaxation strategy followed
with binarization is generally employed to convert it into a continuous-variable optimization
problem. Although rigorous gradient can be obtained, the optimization is generally not so efficient
due to the strong nonlinearity introduced by the binarization and the large number of pixels. A
high degree of binarization introduces strong nonlinearity; but if the binarization is insufficient,
gray pixels cannot be completely removed, rendering the mask unmanufacturable. The smaller
the pixel size, the richer the geometric details that can be represented, and better fidelity can be
reached, but the computational costs increase rapidly.
   In the level-set-based mask optimization [2, 11–17], the contour of mask is represented as the
zero-level set of a level-set function (LSF). During the optimization, a gradient-based optimization
algorithm is used to iteratively update the LSF, thereby implicitly modifying the mask contour.
The value of LSF provides a natural binarization, and thus the optimization is generally more
stable than the pixel-based one. In this method, the design variable is the contour of mask,
which is infinite-dimensional. In numerical implementation, the infinite-dimensional optimization
problem is converted to a finite-dimensional one. However, the number of design variables is still
large, and the convergence rate is generally slow.
   Despite the difficulties mentioned above, the pixel-based and level-set-based curvilinear MO
have achieved great success in past decades. In our view, the success not only comes from their
geometric flexibility but also from the rigorous gradient of the design variables and the subsequent
gradient-based optimization.
   Recently, many efforts are directed toward curvilinear MO with the masks being represented



                                                 2
by parametric curves [18–25]. Since a relatively small number of parameters can describe complex
curvilinear mask contours, the parametric-curve-based mask optimization often has much less
design variables, and thus the efficiency of optimization can be effectively improved.
   However, obtaining the rigorous gradient of an objective function with respect to the design
variables constitutes a major technical difficulty. Generally, the objective function to be minimized
is the edge placement error (EPE) or the L2 norm of the difference between exposed and target
patterns, and the design variables are the position of control points or weights of basis functions. If
the gradient is not available, some gradient-free optimization methods are often employed [21, 24],
for instance the genetic algorithm or particle swarm optimization, but these methods are not so
efficient as the gradient based optimization. Another alternative way to resolve this difficulty is to
use EPE as an approximation of the rigorous gradient [22]. Although this way is effective in many
cases, an EPE only reflects the impact of mask bias at an evaluation point, while ignoring the
influences of mask in the neighborhood around the evaluation point. Actually, this is the reason
why the matrix-OPC (optical proximity correction) via MEEF (mask error enhancement factor)
matrix is more effective than the EPE-based OPC [26–28]. However, because calculation of MEEF
matrix is computationally expensive, it is only used in critical regions.
   In this paper, a general framework is proposed for the parametric curvilinear mask optimization
with rigorous gradient. As an example of the parametric curve, piecewise cubic Bézier curve is
employed to represent the contour of mask. The design variables are carefully selected to compress
their total number, and the design velocity due to the variation of design variables are obtained.
The gradient of the objective function with respect to the design variable is rigorously derived
for gradient-based optimization, which constitutes an important contribution of the present study.
The proposed framework can be used for curvilinear masks represented by any parametric curves
and even for Manhattan-style masks.

mask contour, the design variables should be properly selected.

actively changed during the optimization, and those of the offline knots are passively reconstructed

   The present study provides a general framework for parametric curvilinear mask optimization
with rigorous gradient. As one can see, there are three important links in the derivation of rigorous
gradient. First, using the analytical function of parametric curve, the link between variation of
design variable and variation of mask contour is obtained, and it is called the “design velocity”, as
can be seen in Eq. (12). Second, using the analytical indicator function of the region enclosed by
the mask contour, the link between variation of mask contour and variation of mask transmittance
is obtained, as can be seen in Eq. (30). Third, the rigorous gradient is obtained by the integrating
on the contour the product of gradient of mask transmittance and design velocity, as can be seen
in Eq. (33).

instance the B-spline or non-uniform rational B-Spline. In addition, one can see that the proposed
framework can also be applied to Manhattan-style masks. When an edge Γi in the Manhattan-
style mask is perturbed, the design velocity corresponds to the perturbation is Vn = 1, and the

derivative of the perturbation is simply given by 4 Γi ∇LM dΓ.


4.3. Optimization method

   With the derivative given in Eq. (33), the optimization problem defined in Eq. (24) is solved by
using a gradient-based method. In the present study, the steepest descent algorithm and a simple
strategy to adjust the step size is employed.
   According to the steepest descent algorithm, the design variables ti are updated according to

                                                            ∂L
                                          tk+1
                                           i   = tki − sk                                          (34)
                                                            ∂ti

where the super-script represents the step of iteration; sk is the step size used in the k-th iteration.
The strategy to adjust the step size is: (1) if the value of objective function is increased by a
certain ratio, the step size is reduced. For instance, if Lk ≥ Lk−1 , then sk = 0.6sk−1 . (2) if the
value of objective function is reduced by a certain ratio, the step size is enlarged. For instance, if
Lk ≤ 0.9Lk−1 , then sk = 1.05sk−1 .
   The line search in classical optimization methods is not incorporated into the optimization
because it requires several attempts of step size in each iteration, which needs considerable
computation for lithography imaging simulation.             In our future work, several optimization

methods will be tested in the proposed framework, including conjugate gradient method, BFGS
(Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno) method etc.


4.4. Target pattern

    When the input mask is Manhatten-style, it contains high-frequency features, for instance sharp
corners and jogs, and they will be lost due to the low-pass filtering effect of the projection imaging
system. In practical OPC, such features are not required to be exactly transferred to the silicon
wafer, as long as the EPE at some evaluation points are below an admissible value. Therefore, the
target pattern Z0 can be accordingly modified, which is usually called “target smoothing” [38, 39].
    In the present study, the target smoothing is accomplished by using the cubic Bézier curve, and
the procedure is described in the following. First, classify the edges in the Manhatten-style mask
into jog, line/space end, short segment, and long segment, according to the length of the edge and
direction of its two neighboring edges. Second, for each segment, knots are generated according
to several rules, and we call them “Manhattan knots”. Finally, smoothed target is computed by
using these Manhattan knots.
    It is worth noting that the Manhattan knots are not used to define the design variables in the
optimization, because the lengths of curve segments between such knots are not uniform (recall
the discussion below Eq. (33)). Instead, after the smoothed target is obtained, the curvilinear
contour is uniformly sampled, and these sampling points are used as the online knots. Thereafter,
the offline knots are reconstructed, and the collection of these knots are called “curvilinear knots”.

5. Experimental results

   The proposed method is implemented into an in-house software “LithoLab”.              The C++
and Python programming language are used.            The C++ code is compiled into dynamically
linked libraries, and they are called in the Python code that accomplishes the OPC. The C++
development tools include Mingw-w64 14.2.0 and Boost.Python 1.87.0; OpenMP is used to
parallelize the core algorithms; FFTW 3.3.5 is used for fast Fourier transformation; OpenBLAS
0.3.30 is used to compute TCC and eigenvalue decomposition. Python 3.9.0 and NumPy 2.0.2 is
used to write the OPC scripts. The computer used in the experiments comprises Windows 10,
Intel Xeon Gold 6226R CPU, and 128GB memory.
   The Manhattan-style masks provided in the ICCAD 2013 contest [40] are first scaled by 1.25
and then used as the input of mask optimization. The parameters of the lithography system
include: numerical aperture NA = 0.93, wave length λ = 193nm, annular source with σin = 0.7
and σout = 0.9. Parameters in the resist model are α = 40 and tr = 0.35. In the numerical
computation, the mask is represented by 128 × 128 pixels with the pixel size h = 12 nm (thus
the area of mask is 2.36 µm2 ); the source is represented by 64 × 64 pixels with the resolution
∆fsource = NA/(32λ). The number of TCC kernels is K = 64. The kernels are prepared in a
pre-processing phase and saved in a file, and the time used to compute TCC kernels is 349 ms.
The smoothed target is offset inwardly by 2.5 nm to compute Z0 , and it is rasterized on a grid
refined by 16 times, then the result is down-sampled via the FFT-based interpolation. The initial
step size in the steepest descent optimization is s0 = 1.2, and the optimization is terminated after
20 iterations. All the optimizations are conducted with the same settings.
   The smoothed targets and evaluation points are shown in Fig. 7; the results of mask optimiza-
tions are shown in Fig. 8. As can be seen in Fig. 8, the optimized curvilinear masks are fairly
smooth; the line width and space width are reasonable; the transferred pattern is close to the


                                                15
smoothed target. The histograms of EPEs at evaluation points are shown in Fig. 9.
   The maximum of the absolute value of EPE (denoted as E ∗ ), the objective function L, and the
runtime are provided in Tab. 1. As can be seen, the average of E ∗ is 2.59 nm, and in five of the
ten cases, E ∗ is below 2 nm. In the first four cases, E ∗ is relatively large (above 3 nm), because
the CDs are equal or below 80 nm. In the third case, E ∗ = 4.34 nm is the largest one, because
the CD is the smallest (75 nm). According to the Rayleigh criterion, the resolution of lithography
is R = kλ/NA where k is the so-called process factor. Under ordinary conditions, the value of k
is around 0.4, therefore the minimum line width that can be stably achieved with a 193 nm dry
lithography tool at NA = 0.93 is around 85 nm. This explains why the results of the first four
cases are not so good. In Table 1, the value of L does not perfectly correlate with E ∗ , since L
counts for global difference, but EPE is a local indicator of error.
   For all the ten cases, the optimizations are finished around 2 seconds. According to such
runtime, OPC for a 100 mm2 wafer area with CD around 87 nm can be finished within 24 hours
using 1000 CPUs. Although accurate imaging simulation requires a vector model of the lithography
imaging process and it needs more computation time than the scalar model of the present study,
the results of runtime show the potential for full-chip application.
   Finally, the pixel-based method is also employed for the OPC, with the same settings as those
mentioned before, except that the initial step size is s0 = 0.25. The value of the initial step size
is carefully chosen, and if the value is larger (for instance 0.30) the optimization of many cases
would be quite oscillating or even do not converge. The objective function is L + w(1 − M )M
where (1 − M )M is the penalty to binarize the pixel values M , and w is the weight of binarization
(w = 0.05). The quantitative results of optimization are provided in Table 1. As can be seen, the
runtime used by the pixel-based optimization is less than that of the proposed method, but E ∗ and
L are not so good as those of the proposed method. More importantly, the optimized pixel-based
masks contain many gray pixels, which are not manufacturable.
   The histories of objective function during optimization of the first three cases are shown
in Fig. 10, for both the proposed method and the pixel-based method. As can be seen, the
optimizations by the proposed method converge smoothly, and those by the pixel-based method
are not sufficiently converged. More importantly, one can clearly see that the convergence rate of
the proposed method is higher than the pixel-based method.
   In our view, the advantage of the pixel-based method lies in its ability to generate sub-resolution
assist feature (SRAF) that extends process window, while the advantage of the parametric
curve-based method lies in its efficiency in ensuring the fidelity of transferred pattern. For a



                                                 16
comprehensive OPC solution, the two methods should be intimately combined to deliver both high
fidelity pattern and wide process window. In our future work, the SRAF will be firstly generated,
and then the contour of SRAF and main pattern will be simultaneously optimized by using the
proposed method. If the EPE is used as an approximation of rigorous derivative, the SRAF cannot
be optimized since EPE cannot be defined on SRAF. This is another advantage of the proposed
framework.

6. Conclusions

    Curvilinear mask is flexible in geometric description and offers a broad solution space for optical
proximity correction (OPC). As a result, it can significantly improve the fidelity of transferred
pattern and extend the process window. In this paper, a general framework is proposed for
parametric curvilinear mask optimization with rigorous gradient. As an example of parametric
curve, piecewise cubic Bézier curve is employed to represent the curvilinear contour of mask. The
design variables are judiciously chosen to compress their total number, while maintaining curve
smoothness and geometry flexibility. By employing the concept of design velocity and gradient
density, the gradient is rigorously derived, and a gradient-based optimization is achieved. In
addition, the methods of rasterization, reconstruction of offline konts, and target smoothing are
also presented. With the masks in ICCAD 2013 contest scaled by 1.25 times (average CD 87 nm),
each OPC takes about 2 seconds; maximum EPE remains below 5 nm. Under the same settings of
lithography and optimization, the proposed method outperforms the pixel-based methods in both
